When 'बस 5 मिनट' started getting accepted into festivals, Dad and I attended several in person. I'd never been to a film festival before. I didn't know what to expect.

What I found was a community of storytellers obsessed with craft.

At a festival in Delhi, I watched a 12-minute film by a 16-year-old about his grandmother. It was in a language I didn't speak, but I understood every emotion. At another festival, I saw an experimental piece about urban loneliness that had no dialogue, just sound and color. At a third, a doc about street food vendors moved me to tears.

I realized something fundamental: the best stories aren't about complexity. They're about specificity. Our film worked not because it was clever, but because it was true. We'd captured something real about two people and something in that truth resonated across cultures and languages.

I watched filmmakers receiving feedback from juries and critics. Some got destroyed in respectful ways. Comments like, "The film doesn't know what it's about," or "This moment undermines everything you've built." These weren't cruel—they were precise. They helped you understand what worked and what didn't.

One veteran filmmaker gave me advice that stuck: "Every scene should ask a question or answer one. If it does neither, cut it." This is Buddhist restraint applied to cinema. It's the opposite of YouTube, where more content, more takes, more anything is often assumed to be better.

I started understanding visual language differently. How a camera placed slightly off-center creates unease. How a color palette tells you about a character's emotional state. How matching cuts can create meaning without a word of dialogue.

I also learned how personal filmmaking is. Filmmakers defend their choices fiercely. They're not making content for metrics. They're making art from conviction. That pressure—to make something you believe in, not something that polls well—felt heavier than YouTube, but more satisfying.

I watched documentaries that changed how I see the world. A film about street children in Mumbai made social issues feel personal. A documentary about elderly love made aging seem beautiful instead of sad.

And I started to understand my own film differently by seeing it through others' eyes. Critics focused on moments I'd barely thought about. Audiences laughed at lines I didn't intend to be funny. Some saw sadness where we'd aimed for tenderness.

This variability of interpretation was liberating. With YouTube, engagement metrics tell you if something worked. With festivals, you get nuance. You get debate. You understand that art isn't about universal agreement—it's about resonance.

By late 2022, I'd attended four festivals with our film. I came back changed. I understood that I wanted to keep making things, but not for views or subscribers. For the truth of the story. For the precision of craft. For that moment when an audience member feels seen.

That's what festivals taught me. Storytelling isn't a business model. It's a conversation between maker and viewer about what it means to be human.
