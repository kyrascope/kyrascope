One silver lining of lockdown was finally learning how to edit videos properly. For years, I'd relied on Dad or professional editors to put together my videos. Now, stuck at home with time and a laptop, it was time to level up.

My first lesson: patience. I'd sit for hours with Adobe Premiere Pro open, watching tutorials on YouTube, rewatching my own footage, and making tiny adjustments. A single 10-minute video could take an entire afternoon to edit.

The learning curve was steep. I didn't understand color grading at first. Why did my outdoor footage look washed out? Why did the transitions look jarring? I'd redo sections three, four, five times. Dad would check my progress and gently suggest improvements. "Try a slower fade," he'd say. "This cut is too abrupt."

By September, I was getting decent. I learned about pacing—how to cut out dead air, how long to hold a shot, how to use music to guide emotion. I discovered that editing is where a video actually comes to life. Raw footage is just documentation. Editing is storytelling.

I experimented with effects, though I learned quickly that less is more. Cheesy transitions distract viewers from the content. I started understanding the grammar of video—when to use jump cuts, when to use dissolves, how to build tension before a reveal.

The technical side took time too. Managing files, organizing footage, rendering in different formats. I made mistakes. I corrupted a project file once and lost two hours of work. Another time, I exported a video at the wrong resolution.

But each mistake taught me something. Video editing isn't just a technical skill—it's an art form. It requires creative thinking, problem-solving, and patience. It taught me why the creators I admired put so much effort into their production quality.

By late 2020, I wasn't hiring editors anymore. I could handle simple projects on my own. That independence felt powerful.
