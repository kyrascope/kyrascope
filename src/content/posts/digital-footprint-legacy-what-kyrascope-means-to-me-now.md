I'm cleaning out my room for the new year and I find old journals. Entries from when I was six, seven, eight. I wrote about toy reviews, about videos I wanted to make, about being famous.

Reading them now, I'm struck by how innocent they are. Those were my dreams then: to make cool videos, to get more subscribers, to collaborate with toy brands.

I've achieved those things. So why do I feel like the dreams have changed?

Because they have. Somewhere between age five and twelve, between toy reviews and filmmaking, I went from wanting to be seen to wanting to say something worth seeing.

That's a fundamental shift.

Kyrascope started because my parents thought it would be nice to document my childhood. A family project, really. But it became something bigger—a YouTube channel with thousands of subscribers, a brand identity, a digital footprint that will follow me forever.

There's a surreal quality to that. Everything I've ever posted is permanent. My five-year-old reactions to toys. My awkward nine-year-old attempt at humor. My clumsy early filmmaking. All of it exists forever on the internet, indexed by Google, archived by the internet archive.

For years, that felt like a burden. I'm on camera; I have to be "on." I have to be perfect or at least perform perfection.

But I'm beginning to see it differently.

That digital footprint is a record of learning. It documents not just what I did, but who I was at different points. The five-year-old me loved unconditionally and trusted easily. The nine-year-old me was learning to think critically. The twelve-year-old me is learning that complexity and nuance matter.

When I look at that entire arc—from toy reviews to film festival awards to academic ambitions—it tells a story about growth. About a person who started as an audience for products and became a creator of meaning.

I think that's a legacy worth having.

The downside is real. There are moments when I feel defined by what I've created. People recognize me. Teachers have seen my videos. Future college admissions officers will probably Google my name. I can't untie myself from Kyrascope, even if I wanted to.

But maybe that's okay. Maybe the point isn't to escape it, but to steer it. To decide, consciously, what this platform means and what I want it to represent.

Right now, I want Kyrascope to represent this: a person who thinks about craft. Who tries to make things beautifully. Who uses whatever platform she has responsibly. Who's learning what it means to grow up in public and choosing to do it with intention.

I'm not going to pretend that YouTube views don't matter to me. They do, sometimes. I'm not going to pretend that being featured in publications was exciting. It was. But that's not what drives me anymore.

What drives me now is the possibility that something I make might help someone else feel less alone. Might inspire them to create. Might show them that you can be curious about the world and express that curiosity through whatever medium you choose.

As 2023 closes, I'm grateful for Kyrascope. Not because it made me "famous" or "successful" by conventional metrics. But because it gave me a reason to think deeply about craft, connection, and what it means to show up authentically in a world that often demands performance.

The journey from toy reviews to filmmaking to navigating Class 10 academics has taught me something essential: you get to decide what your platforms mean. You get to decide what legacy you're building.

I'm choosing to build one I'm proud of. One that documents not just views and subscribers, but learning and growth.

That feels like the truest kind of success.
