Looking back, I can see exactly when my creative focus shifted from products to storytelling. It wasn't a dramatic moment. It was quiet.

In mid-2021, Dad and I were talking about a concept. Nothing concrete—just a seed of an idea about relationships, time, and what matters. He mentioned it could become a short film. Not a YouTube video. A real film.

I'd never made anything that wasn't designed for digital platforms. YouTube videos are fast-paced, optimized for mobile screens, built around algorithm retention. Film is different. It's deliberate. It requires discipline.

We started writing. And writing. And rewriting. I was used to scripts for toy reviews—they were functional, maybe a page long. A screenplay is entirely different. Every word has weight. Every scene serves a purpose. Every shot tells something about character or mood.

I borrowed filmmaking books from Dad's shelf. I watched short films on Mubi and Letterboxd—not YouTube's recommendation algorithm, but films chosen by critics and curators. I noticed different things: how directors use silence, how they frame faces, what they choose to show and not show.

For years, I'd been trying to show everything—every angle of a toy, every reaction to a product. In filmmaking, I learned that what you don't show is as important as what you do.

That August and September, we did preliminary shoots. I was nervous in a new way. With toy reviews, if something didn't work, we'd redo it. No stakes. But making a film felt like stepping into something bigger. More real.

I started thinking about acting differently too. As a reviewer, I was myself—enthusiastic, bouncy, camera-aware. As an actor in a film, I had to embody someone else, find nuance, feel emotions fully instead of performing happiness.

Dad brought in collaborators—cinematographer friends, sound designers, other filmmakers. Suddenly I wasn't just working with my family. I was part of a creative community of people who cared deeply about craft.

This transformation was invisible to my audience. To the outside world, I'd simply gone quiet. But privately, I was becoming a filmmaker. I was shedding the identity of "kid YouTuber" and discovering something that felt more authentically me.

The toy reviews had been wonderful. They'd given me a platform, taught me about consistency, built a community. But filmmaking was asking different questions: What do I have to say? What stories matter to me? How do I say something true?

That question would shape everything that came next.
