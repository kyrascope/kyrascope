2021 was the year I learned to live offline—properly, without a camera constantly in hand.

In January, our school finally reopened. I was nervous. I hadn't been in a classroom in almost a year. But walking through those gates felt like coming home. I'd forgotten how much I missed my friends, the chaos of the playground, the bad cafeteria food.

One of my best moments that year was making new friends who had no idea I made YouTube videos. There's a freedom in that. They liked me for me—not because I had an audience. We'd spend afternoons doing homework, gossiping, playing volleyball. It sounds ordinary, but after being a "creator" since age five, ordinary felt revolutionary.

School consumed most of my time, and honestly, I needed that. I joined the debate team (Dad was shocked). I participated in science fairs. I was just another kid in a uniform, not a brand partnership opportunity.

But I didn't completely step away from creating. I took photography seriously that year. I started documenting life authentically—not for content, but for memory. Pictures of my friends laughing, my school courtyard at sunset, our family vacations. I filled my phone with ordinary moments that meant something to me.

I got obsessed with reading. Fiction, mostly. I devoured series I'd been too busy to pick up before. I'd lie in my room with a book for hours, completely transported. No thumbnails, no metadata, no audience watching.

I also started to notice things about the world differently. On a family trip to Agra, instead of filming unboxing videos, I actually looked at the Taj Mahal. I watched how light hit the marble. I thought about the people who built it. I felt small in a way that was beautiful.

I took up badminton seriously. I wasn't performing—I was just playing, trying to get better, enjoying the exercise. That was new.

By the end of 2021, I'd barely posted anything online. But my life felt full in a way it hadn't been since I was five and first walked on camera. I'd been a public figure for so long that I'd forgotten what it meant to just exist.

This year taught me that a life well-lived doesn't require an audience.
